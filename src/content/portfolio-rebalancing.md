## Definition

<div class="def-box">
<p class="definition-text">
<dfn id="rebalancing-def"><strong>Portfolio rebalancing</strong></dfn> is the process of buying and selling holdings to return a portfolio's asset weights to their predetermined target allocation after market movements have caused those weights to drift.
</p>
<div class="def-source">Source: Vanguard Research, <em>Getting back on track: A guide to smart rebalancing</em>, vanguard.com.</div>
</div>

A target allocation is a statement about how much risk an investor intends to carry and where. Prices then move independently of that intention. Holdings that appreciate take up a larger share of the portfolio and holdings that decline take up less, so the risk profile changes without any decision being made.

Rebalancing is the mechanism that converts an allocation from an initial choice into a maintained one.

## How Drift Concentrates Risk

Start with five equal positions of $1,000 each — a $5,000 portfolio at 20% per holding.

<tr><td>Position C</td><td>$1,000</td><td>20.0%</td><td>$1,000</td><td>14.3%</td></tr>
<tr><td>Position D</td><td>$1,000</td><td>20.0%</td><td>$1,000</td><td>14.3%</td></tr>
<tr><td>Position E</td><td>$1,000</td><td>20.0%</td><td>$1,000</td><td>14.3%</td></tr>

</tbody>
</table>
</div>

Position A now carries <data value="42.9">42.9%</data> of the portfolio's risk against an intended <data value="20">20%</data>. The consequence is measurable:

```
If Position A falls 50%:
  At 42.9% weight → portfolio falls 21.4%
  At 20.0% weight → portfolio falls 10.0%
```

The investor never agreed to double the downside from one holding. Appreciation delivered it silently.

Rebalancing this portfolio means selling $1,600 of Position A — bringing it to $1,400, or 20% of $7,000 — and distributing the proceeds across B through E to lift each to $1,400.

## Calendar Versus Threshold

<div class="tbl-wrap">
<table class="dtbl">
<thead>
<tr><th>Method</th><th>Trigger</th><th>Trades per year</th><th>Trade-off</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Calendar — annual</td><td>A fixed date</td><td>Low</td><td>Simple; ignores large drift between dates</td></tr>
<tr><td>Calendar — quarterly</td><td>Four fixed dates</td><td>Moderate</td><td>Tighter control; more trading, more realised gains</td></tr>
<tr><td>Threshold — 5% band</td><td>Weight moves ±5 points from target</td><td>Variable, often zero</td><td>Acts only when risk has actually changed</td></tr>
<tr><td>Hybrid</td><td>Check quarterly, act only if outside band</td><td>Low</td><td>Vanguard's recommended approach for most investors</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>

Vanguard's guidance is to check on a schedule but act on a threshold: review quarterly, and trade only when a holding has drifted more than five percentage points from its target. Its 2024 target-date fund research measured a 15–25 basis point annual advantage for threshold-based strategies over pure calendar rebalancing. Most reviews result in no action, which is the intended outcome.

The reason a rule beats judgement has nothing to do with forecasting. A holding at 42.9% feels most compelling precisely when it has performed best, and a holding at 14.3% feels most doubtful when it has lagged. A pre-committed rule executes the trade that instinct resists.

## How to Rebalance in Practice

**1. Write the target allocation down before you need it.** A target set during a calm period is the only reference point available during a volatile one. Without a written target, "rebalancing" becomes an unconstrained judgement call.

**2. Set the band to match the holding.** Five percentage points is the standard for broad asset classes. For individual stocks in a concentrated portfolio, a tighter band controls single-name risk more effectively; for volatile holdings, a wider band avoids constant trading.

**3. Rebalance with new money first.** Directing new contributions to underweight positions moves the allocation toward target without selling anything, which avoids realising gains entirely. For investors making regular contributions, cash-flow rebalancing handles most drift.

**4. Do the selling inside tax-advantaged accounts.** Trades within a 401(k), IRA or equivalent trigger no tax event. Where an investor holds the same asset classes across taxable and tax-advantaged accounts, executing the rebalance in the sheltered account achieves the same allocation at zero tax cost.

**5. Separate the trim decision from the sell decision.** Rebalancing trims a position to a target weight; it does not exit it. A holding that has tripled and still merits inclusion should end at its target weight, not at zero.

**6. Check why the weight grew.** A position that doubled because earnings doubled is at a similar valuation to where it started; a position that doubled on multiple expansion is not. Cluenex's discounted cash flow and owner earnings estimates address exactly this: whether the price grew into the business or ahead of it. Rebalancing controls the weight either way, but the answer changes how aggressively you trim.

## Common Mistakes and Misconceptions

<div class="callout callout-red">
<div class="callout-label">&#10007; Mistake 1</div>
<p class="callout-text"><strong>"Rebalancing improves returns."</strong><br>
Vanguard's research is direct on this: the return improvement is marginal, and the risk reduction is the real benefit. Over long periods in which one asset class persistently outperforms, rebalancing systematically moves money away from the winner and <em>reduces</em> total return. It is a risk-control tool that occasionally helps returns, not a return-enhancement tool.</p>
</div>

<div class="callout callout-red">
<div class="callout-label">&#10007; Mistake 2</div>
<p class="callout-text"><strong>"I'll rebalance when the market calms down."</strong><br>
Drift is largest after sharp moves, which is exactly when waiting feels prudent. Deferring the trade until conditions feel comfortable means rebalancing after the reversal has already occurred, which delivers the trading costs without the risk control.</p>
</div>

<div class="callout callout-red">
<div class="callout-label">&#10007; Mistake 3</div>
<p class="callout-text"><strong>"Rebalancing means selling my best company."</strong><br>
It means reducing it to the weight you chose. A holding at 42.9% of a portfolio remains the largest position after being trimmed to 20%. The trade addresses position size, which is a risk parameter, and says nothing about the quality of the business.</p>
</div>

<div class="callout callout-red">
<div class="callout-label">&#10007; Mistake 4</div>
<p class="callout-text"><strong>"Taxes make rebalancing not worth it."</strong><br>
Taxes make <em>selling</em> costly, and there are three ways around it: rebalance inside tax-advantaged accounts, direct new contributions to underweight positions, and pair a trim against realised losses elsewhere. Long-term US federal capital gains rates of 0%, 15% or 20% plus the 3.8% net investment income tax are a real cost — but they apply to gains, and they are frequently avoidable through account selection.</p>
</div>

<div class="callout callout-red">
<div class="callout-label">&#10007; Mistake 5</div>
<p class="callout-text"><strong>"More frequent rebalancing is better."</strong><br>
Monthly rebalancing multiplies trading costs and realised gains while controlling risk only marginally better than a threshold approach. Vanguard's finding runs the other way: checking quarterly and acting only outside a 5-point band outperformed calendar-driven trading by 15–25 basis points annually in its target-date fund study.</p>
</div>

## Example: A 60/40 Portfolio Through a Drawdown

An investor holds $600,000 in equities and $400,000 in bonds — a 60/40 allocation on a $1,000,000 portfolio, with a 5-point rebalancing band.

<div class="tbl-wrap">
<table class="dtbl">
<thead>
<tr><th>Stage</th><th>Equities</th><th>Bonds</th><th>Equity weight</th><th>Action</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Start</td><td>$600,000</td><td>$400,000</td><td>60.0%</td><td>None</td></tr>
<tr><td>Equities +15%, bonds +2%</td><td>$690,000</td><td>$408,000</td><td>62.8%</td><td>Inside band — no action</td></tr>
<tr><td>Equities +30%, bonds +2%</td><td>$780,000</td><td>$408,000</td><td>65.7%</td><td><strong>Outside band</strong> — sell $67,200 of equities</td></tr>
<tr><td>After rebalance</td><td>$712,800</td><td>$475,200</td><td>60.0%</td><td>Target restored</td></tr>
<tr><td>Equities then −30%</td><td>$498,960</td><td>$475,200</td><td>51.2%</td><td><strong>Outside band</strong> — buy equities</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>

Without the rebalance, the 65.7% equity portfolio would have entered the 30% decline holding $780,000 in equities and lost $234,000. Rebalanced, it entered holding $712,800 and lost $213,840 — roughly <data value="20000">$20,000</data> less, on a portfolio whose equity exposure had been restored to the level the investor actually chose.

The final row is the part most investors skip. The same rule that trimmed at 65.7% requires buying at 51.2%, into a 30% decline. That trade is where the discipline is genuinely tested, and it is the reason the rule has to exist before the drawdown rather than during it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

- **What is portfolio rebalancing?**
  Rebalancing is selling portions of holdings that have grown above their target weight and buying holdings that have fallen below it, returning the portfolio to its intended allocation. Price movements cause weights to drift automatically, so a portfolio left alone becomes progressively more concentrated in whichever holding performed best.

- **How often should I rebalance my portfolio?**
  Vanguard's guidance is to review quarterly but act only when an allocation has drifted more than five percentage points from its target. Its 2024 target-date fund research measured a 15–25 basis point annual advantage for this threshold approach over fixed-calendar rebalancing. Most quarterly reviews should result in no trades at all.

- **Does rebalancing increase returns?**
  Generally no. Vanguard's research finds the return improvement marginal and the risk reduction substantial. During extended periods in which one asset class outperforms, rebalancing moves money away from the winner and lowers total return. It is a risk-control tool, and evaluating it on returns misses what it does.

- **Why would I sell a stock that is performing well?**
  Because its weight, not its quality, is the problem. A position that grows from 20% to 42.9% of a portfolio carries more than double its intended share of risk — a 50% decline in it would cost 21.4% of the portfolio instead of 10%. Trimming to target reduces that exposure while keeping the position.

- **How do I rebalance without paying capital gains tax?**
  Three approaches. Execute the trades inside tax-advantaged accounts such as a 401(k) or IRA, where no tax event occurs. Direct new contributions to underweight positions, moving the allocation toward target without selling. Or offset realised gains against realised losses elsewhere in the portfolio in the same tax year.

- **What is a 5% rebalancing band?**
  A rule that triggers action only when a holding's weight moves more than five percentage points from its target — a 20% target becomes actionable above 25% or below 15%. The band prevents trading on small fluctuations while ensuring that meaningful changes in risk exposure are corrected.

- **Does my retirement fund rebalance automatically?**
  Target-date funds and most managed or balanced funds rebalance internally on a set schedule or threshold. Individual holdings within a self-directed retirement account do not. The distinction matters: holding a target-date fund means rebalancing is handled, while holding a set of individual funds or stocks inside the same account means it is not.

- **Should I rebalance during a market crash?**
  A threshold rule requires it, and that is the point at which the rule earns its value. A 60/40 portfolio that falls to 51% equities after a 30% decline requires buying equities to restore target — the trade that instinct most strongly resists. This is precisely why the rule must be written before the decline rather than judged during it.
