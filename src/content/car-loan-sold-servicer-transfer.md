## Definition

<div class="def-box">
<p class="definition-text">
A <dfn id="servicing-transfer-def"><strong>loan servicing transfer</strong></dfn> is the reassignment of the right to collect payments and administer a loan from one company to another, without altering the interest rate, principal balance, monthly payment, or term set in the original contract.
</p>
<div class="def-source">Source: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, auto loan and mortgage servicing guidance.</div>
</div>

The company you originally financed a car through is rarely the company you're still paying two years later. Lenders routinely sell the right to service a loan, sell the loan itself, or bundle thousands of loans into a security sold to institutional investors. The borrower's contract does not change. Who administers it does.

## How Auto Loan Sales and Servicing Transfers Work

Dealer financing is a brokered transaction, not direct lending. The dealer's finance office collects an application, credit score, and income, then shops the loan to banks, credit unions, and finance companies such as Capital One Auto Finance, Ally Financial, and Chase Auto. Each lender quotes the dealer a wholesale rate (the "buy rate"). The dealer is legally permitted to mark that rate up before presenting it to the buyer and keep the markup, which is why comparing a dealer's offer against a pre-approved rate from an outside bank or credit union changes the price paid over the life of the loan.

After origination, the lender frequently does not keep the loan on its books. Banks and finance companies pool thousands of auto loans into an asset-backed security (ABS) — a bond whose payments are funded by the monthly payments borrowers make — and sell that pool to investors including pension funds, insurance companies, and money market funds. US auto loan ABS issuance totaled roughly <data value="292">$292 billion</data> in 2025, up <data value="9">9%</data> year over year, within a broader asset-backed securities market that issued around <data value="358">$358 billion</data> across all collateral types, according to SIFMA data. Once a loan sits inside a securitized pool, a separate servicer is typically appointed to collect payments and pass them through to the trust that holds the security, independent of who legally owns the receivable.

This mechanism is why auto lenders such as Capital One are publicly traded companies whose earnings are sensitive to how the loans backing their business perform. Rising delinquency rates inside a lender's loan book are treated by credit analysts as an early signal of stress spreading through middle-income households, well before it shows up in aggregate economic data.

## How to Verify a Servicer Transfer Is Real

1. **Call the new company using a number from its own official website**, not the number printed in the letter you received. Scammers mimic legitimate transfer notices to redirect a payment to a fraudulent account.

2. **Confirm the transfer independently with your original lender** if you still have their contact information. A genuine transfer will show up in both companies' records.

3. **Check your first due date with the new servicer** before assuming your payment date is unchanged. Due dates can shift by a few days during the handoff, and a missed date can trigger a late fee even when the payment was never actually late.

4. **Re-enroll in autopay manually.** Autopay authorizations tied to the old servicer's bank account details typically do not carry over automatically.

5. **Pull a free credit report after the transfer** to confirm the account moved with an accurate payment history and that no gap or duplicate account appears.

6. **Keep records of the original contract.** If a dispute with the dealer ever surfaces, the FTC Holder Rule preserves your right to raise it against whoever now holds the loan, but you need the paperwork to make the claim.

## Common Mistakes and Misconceptions

**"A new company being in charge means my rate can go up."** It cannot. The interest rate, payment, and term are locked into the retail installment contract signed at purchase; the entity servicing or holding that contract cannot unilaterally rewrite it.

**"This means the Truth in Lending Act protects me."** TILA governs disclosures made at loan origination — the annual percentage rate, finance charge, and payment schedule the lender must show a borrower before they sign. TILA's specific 30-day ownership-transfer notice rule (Regulation Z, 12 CFR 1026.39) and the mortgage servicing-transfer notice rule under RESPA (12 CFR 1024.33) apply to mortgage loans secured by a principal dwelling. Neither extends to auto loans. What actually keeps an auto loan's terms fixed after a sale is basic contract and assignment law: under UCC Article 9, an entity that buys or is assigned a retail installment contract takes it subject to the terms already in force.

**"If I bought the car through a bad dealer, I lose my case once the loan is sold."** The opposite is true for goods financed through the seller. The FTC Holder Rule requires dealer-arranged credit contracts to include language making any assignee subject to the same claims and defenses the buyer had against the dealer, up to the amount paid on the contract.

**"Any letter saying my loan was sold must be legitimate."** Fraudulent "your loan has been sold" letters are a documented scam pattern designed to redirect a payment to an account controlled by the scammer. Verify independently before sending money anywhere new.

**"Servicing transfers only happen to subprime borrowers."** Prime borrowers are routed through the same securitization pipeline. A loan's rate tier affects pricing on the ABS market, not whether the loan gets sold or transferred.

## Example: A Transfer Letter Three Weeks After Purchase

A borrower finances a car through a dealer, and the loan is originated by a regional bank. Three weeks later, a letter arrives stating the loan has been sold to a different finance company, with a new address for payments. The borrower has not missed a payment and signed nothing new.

Because there is no federal advance-notice requirement for auto loan sales comparable to RESPA's mortgage rule, this kind of same-month transfer is legal and common — many loans are sold and securitized within the first billing cycles after origination. The borrower calls the new company using the phone number listed on its official website rather than the one printed on the letter, confirms the account number and balance match, and re-enrolls in autopay directly through the new servicer's portal rather than assuming the old authorization carried over. The interest rate and monthly payment on the confirmation letter match the original contract exactly, which is expected: neither party has the legal ability to change them through a sale alone.

## Frequently Asked Questions

- **Does selling my car loan change my interest rate?**
  No. The interest rate is fixed by the retail installment contract signed at the time of purchase. A sale or servicing transfer moves who collects payments; it does not give the new company authority to change the rate, payment, or term.

- **Which law protects my auto loan's terms when it's sold?**
  Basic contract and assignment law under UCC Article 9 requires an assignee to take the loan subject to its existing terms. The Truth in Lending Act's specific ownership-transfer notice rule and RESPA's mortgage servicing-transfer rule apply to mortgages, not auto loans, so no equivalent federal notice-timing statute exists for car loans.

- **Is a "your loan has been sold" letter ever a scam?**
  Yes. Fraudulent transfer letters that redirect payments to a scammer-controlled account are a documented pattern. Confirm any transfer by calling the new company using a phone number from its official website, not the number printed on the letter.

- **What is an auto loan asset-backed security?**
  An asset-backed security (ABS) backed by auto loans is a bond whose payments come from the monthly payments a pool of borrowers make on their car loans. Lenders sell these pools to investors including pension funds and insurance companies, which is why banks that originate large volumes of auto loans are sensitive to loan performance in their stock price.

- **Does the FTC Holder Rule apply to auto loans?**
  Yes, for loans arranged through a dealer. The FTC Holder Rule (16 CFR Part 433) requires dealer-arranged credit contracts to preserve the buyer's claims and defenses against the dealer, and those claims remain enforceable against whichever company later holds or services the loan.

- **How big is the US auto loan ABS market?**
  US auto loan ABS issuance totaled roughly $292 billion in 2025, up 9% year over year, according to SIFMA, within a broader asset-backed securities market of roughly $358 billion across all collateral types that year.

- **What should I do first when I get a servicer transfer letter?**
  Call the new servicer using a number from its official website, confirm your account number and balance match, check your next due date, and manually re-enroll in autopay rather than assuming the old authorization transferred automatically.
