## Definition

<div class="def-box">
<p class="definition-text">
A <dfn id="lockup-period-def"><strong>lockup period</strong></dfn> is a contractual agreement, typically arranged by IPO underwriters, that prohibits a newly public company's founders, employees, and early investors from selling their shares for a specified period after the IPO, commonly 90 to 180 days, after which the restriction expires — an event called a <strong>lockup expiration</strong> or <strong>unlock</strong>.
</p>
<div class="def-source">Source: US Securities and Exchange Commission, Investor Bulletin on Lock-Up Agreements; SpaceX Form 8-K disclosures, 2026.</div>
</div>

When a company sells shares to the public for the first time in an IPO, only a portion of its total shares actually trade — this tradable portion is called the float. The rest of the company's shares, often held by founders, employees, and venture investors, are legally restricted from trading until the lockup expires.

## How Lockups and Unlocks Work

Regulators and underwriters require lockup agreements to prevent a chaotic flood of insider selling immediately after an IPO, which could destabilize the stock before public investors have had time to establish a trading pattern. The restriction gives the market time to settle into a price based on a limited, known supply of tradable shares.

That price, however, reflects only a fraction of the company's total ownership. Stock prices are set by supply and demand: fewer shares available relative to buyer interest tends to support a higher price. When the lockup expires, that supply constraint disappears — sometimes all at once, sometimes in stages.

**SpaceX's 2026 lockup illustrates the mechanics at real scale.** SpaceX, trading under the ticker SPCX, completed its IPO on June 12, 2026, at an offer price of $135 per share, raising net proceeds of approximately $85.7 billion in what was reported as the largest IPO on record — with an initial public float of roughly 638.9 million shares. On August 6, 2026, the company's first lockup tranche expired, making roughly 911.5 million additional shares — held by employees and early investors — eligible to trade for the first time. That single unlock event released more shares than had existed in SpaceX's entire public float since the IPO, expanding the total float to approximately 1.55 billion shares.

SpaceX's lockup is staggered rather than a single cliff date: the August unlock covered the first 20% of eligible insider shares, the company's full 180-day lockup period expires December 8, 2026, and CEO Elon Musk's roughly 6.4 billion shares remain locked separately until June 12, 2027.

## It's Arithmetic, Not a Verdict on the Company

An unlock does not force a stock to fall — it removes a restriction. What happens next depends on how many insiders actually choose to sell and how much new buyer demand exists to absorb the additional supply.

SpaceX's own August 6, 2026 unlock is a useful illustration of this nuance: despite nearly a billion newly eligible shares, the stock closed up 6.1% that day, bucking the conventional expectation that a large supply increase would push the price down. Strong ongoing demand for the shares outweighed the supply increase in that instance.

That said, the historical pattern skews negative more often than not. Academic research on IPO lockup expirations has found average negative abnormal stock returns clustered around expiration dates, alongside a measurable spike in trading volume — a well-documented pattern, even though it does not occur in every individual case, and even though SpaceX's own August 2026 unlock did not follow it.

## How to Use This in Practice

**1. Look up a newly public company's lockup expiration date before buying, or immediately after.** This is disclosed in the IPO prospectus and typically covered in financial news around the IPO itself.

**2. Compare the number of shares becoming eligible to trade against the company's existing public float.** A ratio where the unlock exceeds the existing float, as with SpaceX's August 2026 unlock, signals a larger potential supply shock than a smaller, proportionate unlock.

**3. Check whether the lockup is a single cliff date or staggered across multiple tranches.** A staggered structure, like SpaceX's, spreads the supply increase across several dates rather than concentrating it in one event.

**4. Don't assume a post-unlock price move — in either direction — is a verdict on the company's fundamentals.** A decline may reflect pure supply-and-demand imbalance rather than any change in the business; a rise, as SpaceX showed, can happen despite a very large unlock if demand is strong enough.

**5. Distinguish an unlock-driven price move from a move driven by actual company news.** Check whether the timing coincides with an earnings report, product news, or other fundamental catalyst before attributing a price change to the unlock alone.

## Common Mistakes and Misconceptions

**"An unlock always crashes the stock."** The historical pattern shows an average negative effect around expiration dates, but it is not universal — SpaceX's own August 2026 unlock, despite releasing more shares than the company's entire prior float, saw the stock rise 6.1% that day.

**"A stock decline after an unlock means the company is failing."** A price move driven by a temporary supply-demand imbalance is a different signal than a price move driven by deteriorating fundamentals. The two can look identical on a chart and require different follow-up questions to distinguish.

**"Lockups exist to protect insiders."** They primarily exist to protect the orderly formation of a public market price in the weeks after an IPO, preventing an immediate flood of selling before public investors have had a chance to establish normal trading patterns.

**"All companies use a single lockup cliff date."** Some companies, including SpaceX, use staggered lockup structures that release insider shares in tranches over an extended period rather than unlocking the entire restricted share count on one date.

## Example: Reading SpaceX's Unlock Two Ways

A headline reporting "nearly 1 billion SpaceX shares unlock" on August 6, 2026, could reasonably be read as a warning sign — more shares becoming tradable than existed in the company's public float since its record-breaking June IPO. A trader focused purely on the supply mechanics might have expected downward pressure on the stock that day.

The actual outcome — SPCX closing up 6.1% on the unlock date — shows why the supply event alone does not determine the outcome. Sufficient buyer demand, whatever combination of factors drove it, more than absorbed the new supply. The lesson generalizes: the unlock date tells an investor when a supply shock is possible, not what direction the stock will move. Knowing the date in advance turns what could be a surprising price swing into an event that can at least be anticipated and researched ahead of time — checking demand signals, insider selling disclosures, and company news around the date — rather than reacted to after the fact.

## Frequently Asked Questions

- **What is an IPO lockup period?**
  A lockup period is a contractual restriction, arranged by IPO underwriters, that prevents a newly public company's founders, employees, and early investors from selling their shares for a set period after the IPO — commonly 90 to 180 days.

- **Why do lockup periods exist?**
  They prevent a large, sudden wave of insider selling immediately after an IPO, which could destabilize the stock's price before it establishes a normal trading pattern with public investors. Underwriters typically require them as a condition of managing the offering.

- **Does a lockup expiration always cause a stock to fall?**
  No. It removes a supply restriction, and the price effect depends on how much insider selling actually occurs relative to buyer demand. Academic research finds an average negative effect around expiration dates, but individual cases vary — SpaceX's stock rose 6.1% on its August 6, 2026 unlock date despite releasing more shares than its entire prior public float.

- **How many shares became tradable in SpaceX's first unlock?**
  Approximately 911.5 million shares became eligible to trade on August 6, 2026, exceeding SpaceX's original IPO float of roughly 638.9 million shares and expanding the company's total public float to approximately 1.55 billion shares.

- **How can I find a company's lockup expiration date?**
  It is disclosed in the IPO prospectus (Form S-1 or equivalent filing) and typically covered by financial news outlets in the days surrounding the expiration date for high-profile IPOs.

- **Are all lockup periods the same length?**
  No. While 90 to 180 days is typical, some companies use staggered lockup structures that release different tranches of insider shares at different dates. SpaceX's lockup released 20% of eligible shares in August 2026, with its full 180-day lockup expiring in December 2026, and CEO Elon Musk's shares remaining locked separately until June 2027.
