## Definition

<div class="def-box">
<p class="definition-text">
<dfn id="fundamental-weighting-def"><strong>Fundamental weighting</strong></dfn> is an index construction method that sizes each company's position in a fund by measures of its business performance — sales, profits, dividends, and book value — rather than by its stock market value, in contrast to conventional <strong>market-cap weighting</strong>, which sizes each position by share price multiplied by shares outstanding.
</p>
<div class="def-source">Source: Arnott, R., Hsu, J. &amp; Moore, P. (2005). "Fundamental Indexation." <em>Financial Analysts Journal.</em></div>
</div>

Both methods buy the same underlying universe of stocks. They differ only in how much of the fund's money goes to each company. A market-cap fund automatically gives the largest allocation to whichever company currently has the highest stock market value. A fundamental fund gives the largest allocation to whichever company currently has the highest sales, profits, or dividends — regardless of what its stock happens to trade for that day.

## How the Two Weighting Methods Work

A market-cap index, such as the S&P 500, ranks and weights its roughly 500 constituent companies by market capitalization: share price multiplied by total shares outstanding. When a stock's price rises faster than its business grows, a market-cap fund automatically increases that stock's share of the portfolio, because the weighting formula responds to price alone.

Fundamental indexing replaces price in that formula with accounting measures that change only when a company reports new financials — typically a composite of revenue, cash flow, dividends, and book value. Research Affiliates, the firm most associated with popularizing the approach through its RAFI (Research Affiliates Fundamental Index) methodology in the mid-2000s, argued that because stock prices are noisy and sometimes detached from a company's actual results, a market-cap fund systematically overweights stocks precisely when they are most expensive relative to their fundamentals, and underweights them when they are cheap.

Schwab's Fundamental U.S. Large Company ETF (FNDX) is a widely held example of this approach, tracking the Russell RAFI US Large Company Index. It charges an expense ratio of <data value="0.25">0.25%</data> a year. A comparable market-cap S&P 500 fund, such as Vanguard's S&P 500 ETF (VOO), charges approximately <data value="0.03">0.03%</data> — the fee gap is roughly <data value="0.22">0.22</data> percentage points, or $22 a year on a $10,000 investment, every year, indefinitely.

## Why Fundamental Weighting Costs More

The higher fee is not incidental — it reflects real added costs in how the fund operates.

**More frequent rebalancing.** A company's stock price moves continuously during market hours. Its sales, profits, and dividends update only when it files new financial statements, typically quarterly. A fundamental index therefore holds its weightings fixed between financial reports and then rebalances in a batch when new data arrives, generating more buying and selling activity per rebalance than a market-cap fund, which drifts with prices automatically and needs no active trading to stay in line with the index.

**More complex index construction.** Building and maintaining a fundamentals-based index requires processing standardized financial data across hundreds of companies with different reporting calendars and accounting conventions, which costs more to license and run than simply tracking market value.

**Higher turnover, and in taxable accounts, more potential tax events.** Selling a position that has appreciated to rebalance a fund can realize a capital gain, which in a taxable brokerage account may generate a tax bill for the fund's shareholders even if they never sold anything themselves.

## How to Use This in Practice

**1. Check the expense ratio before comparing anything else.** A 0.22-percentage-point gap compounds. On $100,000 invested for 30 years at a 7% average return, that gap alone is worth tens of thousands of dollars, independent of which fund performs better on a pre-fee basis.

**2. Ask what specific problem the fundamental fund is trying to solve for you.** Fundamental weighting is designed to reduce exposure to stocks that are overpriced relative to their business results. If that is not a risk that concerns you, the extra fee is buying a hedge you may not need.

**3. Look at the fund's actual sector tilt, not just its stated methodology.** Because fundamental weighting anchors to profits and sales rather than price, it tends to underweight fast-growing, richly valued companies relative to a market-cap index — a real, structural difference in what you own, not just in how it is labeled.

**4. Treat historical outperformance claims skeptically.** Backtested results for any weighting scheme are sensitive to the exact time period tested. A period that includes a bubble deflating (like 2000-2002) will flatter fundamental weighting; a period dominated by a handful of extreme-growth winners will flatter market-cap weighting.

**5. Default to the lower-cost market-cap fund unless you have a specific, articulated reason not to.** The burden of proof sits with the more expensive, more complex option.

## Common Mistakes and Misconceptions

**"Fundamental weighting reliably beats the market."** The concept was introduced with backtested results showing outperformance, but the outperformance has narrowed and become inconsistent in the two decades since. Periods dominated by a small number of very large, fast-growing companies — the pattern of the past fifteen years in US large-cap stocks — have favored market-cap weighting, because a pure fundamentals approach underweights those companies until their financial statements catch up to their valuations.

**"A higher expense ratio means a more sophisticated, better strategy."** Cost and skill are not the same thing. A 0.25% fee reflects the operational complexity of the fund, not a guarantee of better after-fee results.

**"Index funds are all basically the same thing."** "Index fund" describes a family of construction methods with materially different costs, holdings, and behavior — market-cap weighting is the default and by far the largest by assets, but it is one design choice among several.

## Example: The Cost Gap on $50,000 Over 20 Years

An investor puts $50,000 into a market-cap S&P 500 ETF charging 0.03% and, separately, considers the same amount in a fundamental-weighted large-cap ETF charging 0.25%. Assuming both funds deliver an identical 8% gross annual return before fees — an assumption made purely to isolate the cost effect, not a performance prediction — the market-cap fund grows to approximately $232,500 after 20 years net of its fee. The fundamental fund, net of its larger fee, grows to approximately $220,700. The roughly $11,800 difference exists entirely because of the 0.22-percentage-point fee gap, with both funds assumed to deliver identical gross returns. Any actual performance difference between the two strategies would add to or subtract from that gap.

## How Cluenex Uses Fundamentals-Based Analysis

Cluenex does not construct index funds. This lets an investor apply the same underlying logic behind fundamental indexing — is this company's price justified by its actual business results — without paying a fund-level fee premium to have that filter applied automatically across an entire index.

The tradeoff is that a fundamental ETF applies its methodology mechanically and continuously across hundreds of stocks at once, while a stock-by-stock approach requires the investor to do the selecting. Neither approach eliminates the judgment call about which companies genuinely deserve their current valuation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

- **What is the difference between market-cap weighting and fundamental weighting?**
  Market-cap weighting sizes each holding by a company's stock market value — share price multiplied by shares outstanding — so bigger, higher-priced companies automatically get a larger share of the fund. Fundamental weighting instead sizes each holding by accounting measures such as sales, profits, dividends, and book value, so a company's weight depends on its business results rather than its current stock price.

- **Is fundamental weighting the same as smart beta?**
  Fundamental weighting is one specific type of smart beta strategy — a broader category of index funds that deviate from simple market-cap weighting using a rules-based methodology. Other smart beta approaches include weighting by low volatility, dividend yield, or momentum.

- **Why does a fundamental ETF cost more than a market-cap ETF?**
  It requires more frequent rebalancing, since accounting fundamentals update only when a company reports financials rather than continuously like a stock price, and it involves more complex index construction and licensing. Schwab's fundamental large-cap ETF charges 0.25% versus roughly 0.03% for a comparable market-cap S&P 500 fund.

- **Has fundamental indexing actually outperformed market-cap indexing?**
  Results depend heavily on the period measured. Backtests from the mid-2000s, when the strategy was introduced, showed periods of outperformance, particularly around the dot-com bubble's deflation. Since then, results have been mixed, and the strategy has underperformed in stretches dominated by a small number of large, fast-growing companies.

- **Should I switch my retirement account from a market-cap fund to a fundamental one?**
  For most investors, a low-cost market-cap index fund remains a reasonable default, and the burden of proof for switching to a higher-fee alternative rests on that alternative demonstrating a clear, sustained edge net of its extra cost. Treat a fundamental fund as a deliberate, informed choice rather than an obvious upgrade.
